# Standard pitch deck outline (max 20 slides)

Grounded in Guy Kawasaki's 10/20/30 rule (20 slides is a ceiling, not a target — 14–18 content slides is the sweet spot; about 20 minutes; fonts of 30pt or larger; dark background on the title slide), Stanford GSB's pitch guidance (tell a story, stay at 1,000 feet, answer "so what?" on every slide, let one person do the talking), Pitch Deck 101's slide-by-slide formula (problem → solution → proof → ask), and Y Combinator's Series A arc (prove it already works, then show it becomes very large).

Four acts map to the four presentation goals. Populate every slide from the project's foundational documents; source markers indicate where each slide's content comes from.

## Act 1 — Grab attention (slides 1–4)

1. **Title** — Project logo, tagline (Message Foundation), presenter name, date. Dark background, minimal text.
2. **Their world** — The industry challenge facing the prospect's segment, stated in the prospect's own vocabulary (ICP doc: persona pain points). Human-centric, not technical. This is the hook — it must read like you understand their business, not like a product ad.
3. **The cost of going it alone** — Fragmentation, duplicated engineering spend, supply-chain/talent/standards risk. No more than 3 bullets, each with a number (LFX data or sourced research).
4. **The project, in one sentence** — The Message Foundation's locked summary verbatim: what it is, why open collaboration is the fix.

## Act 2 — Explain value (slides 5–9)

5. **Why now** — The inflection point making this urgent: market shift, regulation, technology maturity (Message Foundation positioning; sourced research).
6. **The ecosystem** — Adoption footprint: who builds on it, who ships it, scale of the community (LFX data).
7. **How it works at 1,000 feet** — One architecture/scope visual. No deep technical dives; if the prospect wants depth, that is what the appendix and follow-up meetings are for.
8. **Landscape** — Where the project sits relative to alternatives: proprietary options, competing projects, in-house builds — and why it is positioned to win (Message Foundation competitive positioning).
9. **The value of membership** — What members uniquely get: influence, access, visibility, de-risking. The core value proposition slide.

## Act 3 — Build trust (slides 10–14)

10. **Traction** — Contributors, commits, releases, production deployments, growth curve (LFX data). Prove it already works before claiming it gets big.
11. **Case study #1** — A named member: their pain, what they did, the measured result. Approved case studies only.
12. **Case study #2 / member voices** — Second proof point or a wall of approved member quotes.
13. **Who's involved** — Current member logos and governance structure; the neutrality of LF stewardship as a trust signal.
14. **Roadmap** — What's coming and where members shape it. This converts trust into a reason to join now.

## Act 4 — Inspire action (slides 15–18)

15. **Membership tiers & benefits** — Levels, pricing, benefit matrix (published tier data only).
16. **ROI for you** — Tier benefits mapped back to the pain points from slides 2–3, organized by the ICP persona in the room.
17. **Getting started** — The onboarding path: sign, onboard, first-90-days plan. Make joining feel concrete and low-friction.
18. **The ask** — One recommended tier and one clear next step (book the follow-up meeting before leaving the room), plus contact info.

## Slides 19–20 — reserved appendix/backup

Detailed budget, technical FAQ, full member list. Used only in response to questions — never presented proactively.

## Speaker-notes guidance (write into every deck's notes)

- Tell the story of why the project exists rather than listing features.
- Keep to about 20 minutes, leaving the rest of the meeting for discussion.
- Every slide must answer the prospect's "so what?" — if the note can't say why the slide matters to this prospect, flag it.
- Prospect-specific pain-point mapping and suggested talking points go here, not on the slides.
- Follow up within 24 hours.
