# The Linux Foundation Communications Framework — reference and benchmark

Source: two positioning decks shared by Jim Zemlin (LF CEO) in September 2026 — "The Linux Foundation Communications Framework" and the "Linux Foundation Marketing Template" (internal, dated 3/3/20 in the original). These are the LF's own model for how a message platform is built, and this skill's output follows their structure. The LF-level content below is the *example*, not the content to reuse for a project — a project's Message Foundation fills the same slots with its own statements.

## 1. The brand message hierarchy

Four statements, ordered from longest shelf-life to shortest, from most internal to most external:

| Element | LF definition (paraphrased from the deck) | LF's own example | Shelf-life | Primary audience |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Vision** | The long-term reason for being. | "To create the greatest shared technology investment in history." | Longest | Internal |
| **Mission** | Declares the organization's purpose, or why it exists — scope of business and unique competencies. More concrete than the vision; often informs it. | "To be the place for developers and companies to build sustainable open source ecosystems." (longer variant: "To be the organization of choice for the world's top developers and companies to build ecosystems that accelerate open technology development and commercial adoption.") | Long | Internal / industry |
| **Positioning Platform** | Desired role in an industry and relevance to the most important customers. "Positioning platforms bridge the gap between long-term strategy and day-to-day execution and between internal and external audiences." | "Open source powers everything: The Linux Foundation has all the tools to help enterprises get the most out of open source." (shorter variant: "Decentralized innovation. Built on trust.") | Medium | Industry / customers |
| **Tagline** | A creative articulation of the unique value proposition. | "Open source powers everything. Are you ready?" / "Decentralized Innovation. Built on Trust." | Shortest | Customers |

Two things worth copying from how the LF deck teaches this:
- It defines each term *before* filling it in, and shows non-LF examples (a healthcare provider, IKEA) so readers calibrate what a mission statement is.
- It shows the same hierarchy twice — once as definitions, once filled in — so the reader sees the slot and the content side by side. The Message Foundation's §1 table plus Appendix B glossary reproduce this.

## 2. The message matrix ("Building up the brand message layers")

Below the hierarchy the LF deck arranges everything else as one table. Two spanning header rows (Mission Statement, Positioning Statement), then one column per business line, then four stacked rows:

| | PROJECTS | EVENTS | TRAINING | COMMUNITY / SERVICES |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Value Propositions** | We are the home of professional open source. We build open source ecosystems. | LF events offer breadth and depth for both technologists and business people. | Our training provides you with the skills needed to succeed with open source. | Our services help companies manage open source strategically. |
| **Key Messages** | We host the world's most important projects. | Our events are the place where top developers and users of open source meet. | Our training programs meet the demand for OSS skills from the source. | Our community helps companies manage open source strategically. |
| **Supporting Points** | 250+ projects in addition to Linux; 1,600 member companies; millions of lines of code. | 40,000 users, developers and DevOps pros at 100+ events a year; proceeds go back to the communities. | Hands-on, skills-based certifications; training straight from the source. | Manage "external R&D"; manage IP risk; custom software to enable development. |
| **Sound Bites** | "We want to be the best upstream for a commercial downstream." "The world's technology infrastructure depends on the work being done at Linux Foundation." | "The Linux Foundation knows developer events better than anyone else." "The developers and buyers of technology meet at Linux Foundation events." | "The Linux Foundation helped get my latest job." "Learn the hottest technologies straight from the source." | "The LF is a neutral party whose intentions are clear about accelerating open technology development and adoption." |

The deck's definitions for the four row labels (reproduced in the template's Appendix B):

- **Positioning** — "the conceptual 'space' a company occupies in the mind of the customer. It is rooted in competitive advantage and how that advantage is expressed to the customer. Positions must resonate with broad audiences, yet motivate and influence individuals on their own terms."
- **Value Propositions** — "Derived from the concept represented by the positioning, the value propositions specifically define the ultimate benefits 'promised' to the customer. As promises, value propositions are based on both rational and emotional variables."
- **Key Messages** — "the concise expression and articulation of a company's desired and/or real positioning to a target audience. Typically limited to three or four, key messages should express leadership and points of differentiation."
- **Proof Points** — "the important and essential elements that reinforce and validate a company's messages and position. Proof points are tangible examples of how a company has, or will establish, its position."
- **Sound Bites** — "illustrate how key audiences might 'play back' a company's position and key messages. These creative expressions of the messages also help increase a spokesperson's 'quotability.'"

The deck's own summary line: "Strong brand platforms are simple, yet powerful."

For a single project the columns are usually messaging pillars rather than business lines; for an umbrella foundation with distinct offerings (projects, events, training, certification, membership) the business-line columns fit better. Ask the user which framing they want if it isn't obvious.

## 3. What the downstream decks consume

Jim also shared five decks built *from* messaging like this — two member executive briefings (Oracle, IBM/Red Hat), the "Where the world builds together" LF narrative deck, a DPI/OpenWallet conference keynote, and the CNCF Governing Board deck. Read together they show exactly which Message Foundation sections a deck-building agent reaches for. Each observation below maps to a template section.

**Stat bank with provenance (template §10).** Every number on every slide carries a label, a source, an as-of date or window (typically "Sept 1 2025 – Aug 30 2026" or "as of Sept 1 2026"), and a caveat where needed: "every figure here is a floor," "matched on oracle.com accounts only," "a company joining three foundations counts three times," "Only projects onboarded to LFX Meetings are counted." Figures are visibly one of: live LFX data, published annual-report data, or third-party (Harvard/Nagle, PwC, SlashData, VulnCheck, analyst reports). The dominant slide grammar is **four hero numbers + one-sentence thesis + an em-dash kicker + a Sources footer**, reused a dozen times per deck — so the Foundation pre-packages "big four" sets by theme (Scale, Momentum, Community, Return).

**Persona-specific angles and ROI (template §6a).** The briefings frame the same facts differently by reader: CFO ("<0.1% of a large enterprise's annual R&D budget," "2× the productivity benefit for firms that contribute versus firms that only consume"), CTO ("governance follows code: where you maintain the most, you also chair, vote, and set direction"), legal/policy (JDF IP framework, ISO fast-track, EU CRA), developer ("nobody answers to us"), regional (LF Europe: "local ownership, global reach"). The CNCF AI Conformance deck plants executive questions: "Is your platform Kubernetes AI Conformant?" / "Are we building on the standard AI API or something custom?"

**Pillar tags and goals vocabulary (template §7).** The CNCF marketing update buckets every piece of H1 content under three named narratives — `AI Narrative`, `Project + Community Momentum`, `Brand & Community` — and tags every activity "Supports: Brand Visibility · Member + Event Growth · Community Content · Project Adoption · Education Growth." It also names flagship projects as "living proof" of a pillar (llm-d and OpenTelemetry for the AI narrative) and states a tagline-ownership plan: "Own 'Where AI systems run' as the industry's shorthand… consistent language across keynotes, speakers, and paid media."

**Origin story and before → after cases (template §11).** The narrative deck states a repeatable three-step pattern — "a contested or closed market, one donation into neutral governance, and a decade later an industry default that nobody owns" — then runs it through twelve markets as BEFORE/AFTER pairs (Kubernetes, ONAP/Nephio, PyTorch, AV1, FINOS, LF Energy, Node.js, Hyperledger, Zephyr…), a scoreboard recap, and a "why the pattern keeps repeating" economics slide. The IBM deck closes its security section with four victory conditions and one sentence defining victory.

**Objections, threats and stance (template §12).** The IBM briefing devotes a third of its length to "Five challenges in open source AI" — each a divider with a one-sentence thesis, then evidence, then the LF's answer and an ask. The narrative deck has an explicit honesty posture: "We publish our own bad news — and act on it," with declining metrics stated plainly and a live at-risk list.

**CTA library (template §14).** Asks appear at every commitment level and are audience-specific: join a user group (ANDOR), submit a project (ContextForge), upgrade a tier (the "what the big three hold that you don't" list), fund a program (Akrites, an ISRG grant), co-build (an open harness). The narrative deck's closer is a stat drumroll plus "Membership is how you get a seat, a vote, and a hand on the direction."

**Voice evidenced in the decks (template §3).** Declarative full-sentence headlines that make a claim; the number first, then the gloss, then the kicker; exact figures in body copy and rounded in headlines; no superlative without a qualifier and a source ("largest end user network of any open source software foundation"); occasional plain-spoken humor ("1 — Linus Torvalds — the number of in-house developers people can name"); the word "neutral" as the load-bearing adjective.

**Sound bites the decks lean on** (LF-level examples, for calibration only): "The neutral room is the product. Nobody else can host it." · "The dues don't buy code — your engineers write the code. The dues buy the neutral place where two thousand other companies' engineers write it with you, and the seats where the rules get set." · "The reason a competitor will build on your project is precisely that you do not own it." · "Agreement is the scarce resource." · "Members write the code; dues pay for everything that is not code." · "The standards body you never heard of." · "Open weights without open data is half a movement."

## 4. How to use this file

Read it before generating so the bar is calibrated: the hierarchy in §1 and the matrix in §2 are the *shape* the Message Foundation must take; the observations in §3 tell you what downstream agents will look for and why each template section exists. Do not copy LF-level statements into a project's document — every slot is filled from that project's own interview, Brand Kit, README and sources, or marked TBD.
